What was measured

Method

Each scenario is one chaos-injected fault from RCAEval RE2 — a real failure in Online Boutique, Sock Shop, or Train Ticket. Its observed telemetry is seeded into a knowledge graph and a vector store; a multi-agent system is then asked to diagnose it three times, under three retrieval conditions.

The task is fault-type diagnosis for a known affected service. The benchmark supplies the faulted service, so the system is asked why it failed, never which service failed. Nothing here measures root-cause localisation, and no result should be described as finding the culprit service.

Every diagnosis is split into atomic claims, and each claim is judged twice — once by self-consistency (does it recur across independent samples?) and once by GPCS (can it be traced to retrieved graph evidence above a fixed 0.30 relevance floor?). Both verifiers judge the identical claim list. Neither the floor nor the 0.50 trust threshold is calibrated — both are hand-set defaults, never fitted on a held-out split.

Findings

Each carries its evidential status

F1Significant

GPCS is substantially stricter than self-consistency

Δ unsupported-rate = +0.119  ·  95% CI [+0.073, +0.163]  ·  Wilcoxon p < 0.0001  ·  n = 36 scenarios (clustered)

GPCS flags 70.3% of claims as unsupported; self-consistency flags 57.9%. The two agree on 65.5% of claims.

The bootstrap is clustered on scenario, not on scenario × condition. The three conditions of one incident share the same seeded evidence and the same graph, so their deltas are correlated; treating all 108 as independent is pseudo-replication that narrows the interval on what is really a 36-incident sample. Clustering widens the CI from [+0.087, +0.152] to [+0.073, +0.163] — the effect survives, at the width the design actually supports.

They also disagree asymmetrically: in 864 cases GPCS flags a claim the ensemble was confident about, versus 407 the other way. A claim can be repeated identically by every sample and still have no evidence behind it — which is precisely the failure mode self-consistency cannot see.

F2Null result

Retrieval context does not change what GPCS accepts

GPCS unsupported: none 70.0% · raw 70.1% · hybrid 70.9%  —  spread 0.9 pp over ~1,200 claims each
hybrid vs raw concordance Δ = +0.024 · 95% CI [−0.028, +0.077] · p = 0.302

Showing the agents no evidence, all the evidence, or ranked evidence makes almost no difference to how much of their output is graph-grounded. The confidence interval straddles zero.

This is coherent rather than disappointing: GPCS scores claims against the graph, and the graph does not change with what the generator was shown. It does mean the retrieval-context ablation, as designed, cannot demonstrate value — the verifier is measuring something the manipulation doesn't touch.

F3Significant

Neural retrieval beats symbolic retrieval — and keyword never once succeeds

recall Δ (hybrid − keyword) = +0.190 · 95% CI [+0.116, +0.269] · p = 0.0003 · n = 36

Under strict scoring — every expected tag retrieved — keyword search scores 0 out of 36. Not once in thirty-six scenarios did it recover a complete tag set, and its recall was exactly 40% in all six independent batches.

The shipped correct metric reports 36/36 for all three methods, because it needs only half the expected tags and the service name alone satisfies that. It is saturated by construction and hides the entire effect.

F4Null result

The graph adds nothing to retrieval

vector ≡ hybrid on strict accuracy, recall, and per-fault breakdown — identical in all 36 scenarios and all six batches

Hybrid retrieval never separated from pure vector search on any measure. This is reported as a negative result: on this benchmark the graph traversal contributes nothing to finding evidence.

It does contribute to scoring it. Correcting a defect where evidence with no graph path received full proximity credit moved GPCS's unsupported rate by 13 points and turned a near-binary trust score into a graded one — so the graph matters where provenance is judged, not where candidates are fetched.

F5Deterministic

Retrieval fails on vocabulary, not on difficulty

Breaking strict accuracy down by fault type splits the benchmark almost perfectly in two — and the dividing line is lexical.

This is not retrieval being weak on hard faults. It is a benchmark-vocabulary mismatch: the label uses a word the telemetry does not contain. It reproduced exactly across both independent replicates of every fault-type pair, on disjoint cases — retrieval is deterministic given seeded data, so these are not sampling artefacts.

F6Reproducible

Groundedness tracks claim type

Claims asserting a concrete relationship between named entities are grounded four times more often than vague general statements. 91.6% of "general" claims have no evidence at all — and that figure landed between 88.5% and 94.2% in every one of the six batches.

This is what a provenance-based verifier ought to do: reward specificity, refuse to certify hand-waving.

F7Observational

Which system you diagnose matters more than how you retrieve

Per-scenario variability is also strongly asymmetric between the two verifiers: GPCS ranges from 33.7% to 89.2% (sd 12.7), self-consistency only from 47.4% to 69.6% (sd 6.4). GPCS discriminates between incidents; self-consistency largely does not.

F8Null result

Neither verifier's flags track whether a claim is correct

base rate of incorrect claims = 68.4%  ·  GPCS precision 0.681  ·  self-consistency precision 0.681  —  flagging everything scores 0.684
evaluable subset n = 155 (4.2% of claims)

Both verifiers post respectable-looking precision. Both are also sitting exactly on the base rate, which is what a verifier that flags everything would score. The discriminating question is not precision but whether the flag rate differs between claims that are right and claims that are wrong.

Correctness here is labelled automatically from RCAEval's own case metadata — the faulted service and injected fault type — and only for causal claims, since a descriptive observation such as "exhibited increased memory usage" may be perfectly true during a CPU fault.

How much weight this carries. Coverage is narrow (4.2%) and biased toward causal claims with a nameable mechanism, and the labels remain imperfect — a claim citing "CPU and memory pressure" on a CPU fault is scored contradicted. Label noise attenuates any real signal toward zero, so this is evidence against a strong effect rather than proof of none. It did, however, survive three successive versions of the labeller whose error rates differed substantially (+1.1, +2.8, −0.8 pp), which is the main reason to take it seriously.

Building that labeller was itself instructive: the first version read "CPU spike caused degradation to orders service" as blaming the orders service, and the second read "stable CPU rules out CPU saturation" as blaming CPU. Both produced confident, plausible precision figures from labels that were wrong on inspection.

Four defects that produced confident, invalid results

Evaluation integrity

Four separate runs of this evaluation completed cleanly, produced plausible numbers, and were wrong. Each defect was caught by a different check, and none would have been visible in the results themselves.

All four are now enforced in code rather than by discipline: scenario-scoped retrieval in both stores, a fatal pre-run isolation assertion, a dedicated evaluation namespace, and a merge step that aborts on duplicate claims, broken joins, or any claim reproducing held-out ground truth.

A related measurement is worth reporting on its own terms. Four isolated re-runs of the same six scenarios moved per-condition concordance across a 12-point range, with the best-performing condition changing three times. At temperature 0.8, single-batch condition comparisons are noise.

What these results do not show

Agreement is not accuracy. Concordance measures whether two verifiers reached the same verdict, not whether either was right — both can be wrong on the same claim and it counts as agreement. Held-out ground truth is used only to prove the absence of leakage; nothing in this dataset scores a claim as true or false.

So F1 establishes that GPCS is stricter, not that it is better — and F8 finds no evidence that the extra strictness is better aimed. On the subset that can be labelled automatically, GPCS's higher flag rate is a higher flag rate, not sharper discrimination. Settling that properly needs human-labelled correctness, which is the single most valuable piece of work still outstanding. Everything here is scoped to RCAEval RE2 and should not be read as a general result about Kubernetes root-cause analysis.

Conclusion

What holds

Two verification signals that are routinely treated as interchangeable proxies for reliability disagree on more than a third of claims, and disagree asymmetrically. Graph provenance rejects a great deal of what an ensemble confidently repeats. That is a measurable, statistically supported dissociation, and it holds across three systems and six fault types.

What neither of them appears to do is track correctness. On the claims that can be labelled against the benchmark's own metadata, both verifiers flag right and wrong answers at indistinguishable rates. The dissociation in F1 is therefore between two signals, not between a better one and a worse one — a finding that is uncomfortable for the method and, precisely for that reason, worth reporting.

The retrieval comparison is cleaner still, because it has objective ground truth. Symbolic search recovers the service name and reliably misses the mechanism — 0 of 36 complete retrievals. Neural search does substantially better, and the graph adds nothing on top of it. The failures that remain are lexical, not semantic.

The most transferable finding may be the least glamorous one. Four consecutive runs of a carefully built evaluation produced confident, plausible, invalid numbers, and every one was caught by an integrity check rather than by the results looking wrong. Agent evaluations fail silently by default — which is an uncomfortable thing to learn while building a system whose purpose is to catch agents failing silently.
